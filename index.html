Questions drivers ask after a crash

Where we help

Crashes we handle: car, Uber and Lyft, motorcycle, truck, pedestrian and bicycle. Our office is in Los Angeles. We serve Los Angeles, Beverly Hills, Santa Monica, Culver City, Westwood, Pasadena and Long Beach. Consultations by phone or by appointment.

01Do I pay anything to start my case?

No. We work on a contingency fee, which means there is no attorney fee unless we win your case. Our fee is a percentage of your settlement or verdict. You are responsible for case costs we advance, such as medical record fees, even if there is no recovery. The fee agreement explains this before you sign.

02Does sharing the blame end my claim?

No. In California, a driver who was partly at fault can still recover under pure comparative negligence. The amount is reduced by that driver’s percentage of fault.

03What is the time limit after a car accident in Los Angeles?

For most car accident injury claims, California sets a two-year limit for filing a lawsuit, counted from the crash. There are exceptions. If a city, county or state agency was at fault, a written government claim has to be filed much sooner, within months. Ask about deadlines promptly.

04What if I was in an Uber or Lyft crash?

Rideshare crashes can involve the driver’s personal policy and the coverage Uber or Lyft carries. Which one applies usually depends on whether the app was on and whether a ride was underway.

05Can we do this in Spanish?

Yes. Our intake staff speak Spanish; they are not attorneys. Ask for Spanish when you call, or tell us when we contact you. Nuestro personal de admisión habla español.

A note for the review. This part isn’t on the real page. It’s here so you can see our thinking.

Why we built it this way

We didn’t guess at this page. We looked at how people actually behave on Colony’s site, and at how the other firms in Los Angeles go after the same click. What follows is what we found, what we did about it, and how sure we are.

Each decision below has a number next to it. That’s how confident we are that the evidence backs it up. It isn’t a promise about leads or cases. Anything we weren’t at least 80% sure about stays out of the list and shows up near the end, labeled as a test.

What we looked at

Your own numbers from July to November: Google Analytics, Microsoft Clarity and CallRail. The Google Ads account, including keyword quality scores and landing page experience. Six Los Angeles injury firms, page by page, looking at layout, claims, speed, forms and calls to action. And an independent review of eleven Los Angeles landing pages that we read alongside our own. We couldn’t always see the exact page a firm sends its paid clicks to, so some of what we studied is their public site.

What we’re sure about

  1. 85%

    A headline in the searcher’s own words

    People type “car accident lawyer Los Angeles.” Your ads say the same thing. The old page opened with a city hero instead, so the click and the page weren’t saying the same thing. The strongest paid pages in the independent review match the search. Only one of the six firms we looked at does, which leaves that spot open for you.

  2. 90%

    The phone number is the biggest thing on the page

    Same tracking code, two very different pages. On the Dallas car page, about one visit in four ends in a phone tap. On the LA page, since late October, it’s fewer than one in a hundred. Every firm we looked at puts a call button in the first screen, and about two thirds of your paid visitors are on a phone. So the number is large, and on a phone it follows you down the page.

  3. 90%

    The fee line sits right next to the ask

    Five of the six firms keep it there, and so do the strongest pages in the independent review. We used the wording from your own vetted page, costs disclosure included. Counsel should still give it a final read.

  4. 90%

    Only proof you can back up

    4.9 on Google, 175+ reviews, and Kevin’s real background. No results, no percentages, no hours. Those are the claims that get firms in trouble, and the independent review flagged them as the riskiest. The review count comes straight from your live page as of October.

  5. 90%

    A page that loads fast

    It’s about 250 KB. The firms we looked at run from 0.6 MB to 5 MB. Most of your paid keywords are rated below average for landing page experience in Google Ads right now, so speed isn’t a nice extra here.

  6. 80%

    Kevin and the team are in the first screen

    None of the six firms puts its team next to a named lawyer up top. The independent review found that people with names beat anonymous scale. That’s why Kevin’s name sits on the team photo.

  7. 85%

    The page says what the ad says

    Your ads lead with “talk to us before you talk to their insurer,” so the page says it right back. When the page matches the ad, people don’t feel like they landed somewhere else.

  8. 80%

    We ask first, then explain

    The independent review found that paid pages that turn into articles lose the click. Your visitors already read for minutes and rarely act. So the ask comes early and the explaining comes after.

  9. 80%

    Three short steps

    The pages the independent review rated highest keep the process to three short steps. It answers “what happens if I call?” without a wall of text.

  10. 80%

    A call and form bar on phones

    It shows up once the first form scrolls out of view, so there’s always a button under a thumb. The strongest paid page in the independent review does the same thing.

  11. 95%

    It works for everyone

    This is the one we measured instead of estimated. Lighthouse scores it 100 for accessibility on phone and desktop.

How it looks, and why

One typeface, set big. Someone who just got in a crash is stressed and probably on a phone. One clean typeface, large and tightly spaced, lets them get the point in a couple of seconds.

Ink, white, and a little gold. There’s no decorative color. Gold shows up only where something matters: the line under the phone number, the final step, a few numerals. The only real color on the page is the photograph of your team, which is how it should be.

The ask first. The call number and a two-field form are in the first screen, with the fee line and consent wording right under them. Reviews and Kevin’s background back the ask up. They don’t get in front of it.

The whole team. The people who’ll answer the phone are shown together and uncropped, with Kevin named. We feathered the edges of the cut-out so it reads as one scene.

One line down the middle. “What happens next” is symmetrical on purpose. Three equal steps show whose move it is: you, Colony Law, you. Then three equal prompts show what to say on the call. A worried person wants things to look orderly, and symmetry does that.

Small things that prevent mistakes. The phone number formats itself as you type. Errors say exactly what’s wrong. The confirmation reads the number back and lets you fix it, so nobody gets a call on the wrong number.

Built for everybody. Strong contrast, clear focus, full keyboard use, reduced motion for people who ask for it, light and dark themes, and a print version that keeps the phone number and the fee terms.

Some of this is plain design judgment: the typeface, the colors, the spacing. We think it helps and we’d say about 70% on it, so none of it is in the list above.

What we want to test

We think these will help, but we don’t know yet, so we’re not claiming them.

  • Two form fields instead of seven. The live form has seven, and the firms we studied run from two to seven or more with no pattern. We expect fewer to help, and we’ll measure it.
  • Calling first versus filling in the form first. We’ll build the other version and run them against each other.
  • The whole team versus one portrait of Kevin.

What we left out on purpose

Case results and dollar amounts, success-rate percentages, “24/7” and response-time promises, client quotes and staff names. These are the riskiest claims under California advertising rules, and the evidence we have doesn’t call for them. If Kevin and counsel approve one verified result with its disclaimer right beside it, there’s a spot for it next to the ask.

We’ll look at phone taps and form submissions per paid visit in Google Analytics, calls over 60 seconds in CallRail, and signed cases. With the paid click volume you have now, a clear answer takes a few weeks. Until then we’ll watch Clarity for the early signs: how far people scroll, how many leave right away, and how many tap to contact you.
